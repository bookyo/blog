## Body (written after ending)

### The Moon's Gravity Is Not Strong Enough — But It Is Enough

The moon's pull on Earth's oceans is weaker than the gravitational force holding your coffee mug to the table. But it raises tides twice a day, every day, for four billion years. The trick is not strength — it is gradient.

The near side of Earth is only 6,371 km from the planet's center, but the moon is 384,400 km away. That means the ocean on the near side is 1.7% closer to the moon than Earth's center, and the ocean on the far side is 1.7% farther. Gravity does not care about percentages this small — but the difference in pull does. A 3.4% differential across a planet's width raises two bulges of ocean, one facing the moon and one directly opposite.

**Why two bulges?** Newton proposed this in 1687 and it is still counterintuitive. The near-side bulge makes intuitive sense — the moon pulls the ocean toward itself. But the far-side bulge seems to demand that something is pushing the water away. That something is Earth's own inertia. As Earth orbits the moon (they both orbit their common center of mass), the ocean on the far side lags behind, creating a second bulge diametrically opposite the first. Earth rotates inside this two-bulge system, which is why most coastal locations experience two high tides and two low tides per day.

### The Spring and Neap Cycle — The Sun Joins the Argument

The moon is the primary tidal driver, but it is not the only one. The sun generates its own tidal force, roughly 46% as strong as the moon's — about 5 × 10⁻⁸ m/s² at Earth's surface. That gap sounds small until you see it in action once a month.

When the sun, moon, and Earth align — at new moon and full moon — the two tidal forces add together. The ocean rises higher than usual, sometimes by a meter or more on open coasts. These are spring tides, from the Old English *springan*, meaning to swell. When they align at perigean spring tide — when the moon is at its closest approach to Earth — the combined pull can raise tides two meters above normal. The Bay of Fundy has recorded ranges above 16 meters under these conditions.

When the sun and moon sit at right angles relative to Earth — at first and third quarter — the sun's pull partially cancels the moon's. Neap tides result: lower, gentler, less dramatic. Most harbor workers and fishermen have an intuitive sense of which weeks to avoid going out. The spring-neap cycle is an arithmetic fact that plays out as a physical spectacle along every coastline on Earth.

The spring-neap cycle is a clean example of constructive and destructive interference in a system that most people encounter as a visual spectacle rather than an arithmetic one. The same ocean responds differently depending on the geometry of three bodies.

The ratio of roughly 2.2:1 explains why the moon appears in every culture's tidal folklore and the sun does not — the moon is not just closer, it is mathematically dominant in this specific ratio, and the ratio is what drives the height of the bulge.

You can also test what happens when the moon is at perigee versus apogee — when it is closest to Earth versus farthest. At perigee, the tidal force is about 20% stronger than average. Combined with a spring tide, perigean spring tides can raise sea level a meter or more above normal. The inverse is true at apogee: neap tides during apogee produce the gentlest tidal range of the lunar month.

### The Physics You Cannot See From Shore

Tidal forces are weakest where the water is shallow and strongest where it is deep. In the open ocean, the tidal acceleration acts on a full water column and the bulge propagates freely. Near coastlines, friction against the seabed slows the response and distorts the timing. The equilibrium tide — what you would see if the ocean responded instantly and without friction — is not what you measure at a harbor.

This is why coastal tides often arrive hours after the theoretical lunar schedule, and why the amplitude bears no simple relationship to the moon's distance. The port of Shanghai experiences a tidal range of roughly 3 meters on average days. The Bay of Fundy, a narrowing funnel of ocean between New Brunswick and Nova Scotia, regularly sees ranges above 13 meters and has recorded 16.3 meters during perigean spring tides. The geometry of the bay acts as an amplifier: the incoming tide is forced into a channel that narrows from 80 kilometers to 40, and the water piles up.

The same physics that gives you a 3-meter tide in Shanghai gives you a 16-meter tide in Fundy. The moon did not change. The coastline did.

### Why the Gradient Matters More Than the Pull

The central insight of tidal physics is that gravity's variation across an object — its gradient — creates forces that the absolute gravitational field alone cannot. This is the same physics that governs every orbiting binary system, every moon-raising planet, and every star caught in the gravitational field of a neighbor.

What makes tides remarkable is not their strength but their persistence. The moon has been doing this for over four billion years. Earth rotates beneath it, and the ocean, which covers 71% of the planet's surface, has been stretched and released in a continuous cycle for longer than any coastline has existed in its current form.

The tidal force is a small pull. It is also the most reliable timekeeper on the planet — one that runs without batteries, without adjustment, and without apology. When you next watch the water reach high tide and then retreat, you are watching the differential gravitational pull of a rock 384,000 kilometers away, expressed as a two-meter rise in sea level along a coast.

The equation for the tidal acceleration is:

$$a_{\text{tidal}} \approx 2G \frac{M_{\text{moon}}}{D_{\text{moon}}^3} R_{\text{earth}}$$

G is the gravitational constant. M is the mass of the moon. D is the distance from Earth to the moon. R_earth is Earth's radius. The result is a tiny number — about 1.1 × 10⁻⁷ meters per second squared at the surface — but it acts continuously, on every molecule of ocean, for billions of years. The small force accumulates into the most reliable rhythm on Earth.

The tidal force is not a pull. It is a stretch — a consequence of the fact that gravity is never uniform across any real object. Every point on Earth feels a slightly different pull toward the moon. That differential, not the absolute pull itself, creates the bulge.

Newton calculated this differential with a pen and a sheet of paper in 1687. His result — that the moon raises two bulges on opposite sides of Earth — was controversial for decades. Many astronomers argued against it until the 18th-century voyage of HMS Beagle, when the ship's naturalist Charles Darwin (not yet the evolutionary biologist) used tidal observations to map the behavior of the ocean along the South American coast. Darwin's maps matched Newton's prediction exactly. The two bulges were not a theory — they were a measurement.

The moon wins not because it pulls hardest, but because it changes its pull most sharply across Earth's width. The sun contributes but cannot compete alone. And every tide you have ever watched, whether from a cliff in Cornwall or a pier in Tokyo, is a local expression of that global stretch — the ocean rising and falling in answer to a differential force that is invisible and irresistible, and that has been running without interruption since the ocean first formed.
