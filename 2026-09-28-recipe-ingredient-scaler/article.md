## The density table is where most home cooks lose grams

Most recipe scalers stop at finding the first number and treating it as the quantity. This one looks at four pieces per line — and gets three of them right even when the input is messy. The tool ships with a built-in density table of 70+ common baking ingredients — flour at 120 g per US cup, butter at 227 g per cup, granulated sugar at 200 g per cup, brown sugar (packed) at 213 g, honey at 340 g, and so on. The table is the single source of truth for the cups↔grams conversion, and it is also the reason the metric output of `2 1/4 cups all-purpose flour` lands at `540 g` instead of guessing. When the ingredient is not in the table — say, `1 cup ground flaxseed` — the tool degrades gracefully: it keeps the original volume unit and flags the line with a `density-unknown` marker. No fabricated grams, no silent fallback to a default ingredient. The fallback policy is conservative on purpose; guessing density for an ingredient the tool has never seen is how `1 cup of cocoa powder` becomes 80 g of cocoa instead of the actual 75 g. Brown sugar is the one that trips people up. Packed vs unpacked brown sugar is a 15 percent density difference, and the table has both rows. If you paste `1 cup brown sugar, packed`, the parser reads the comma qualifier and routes to the packed row. If you paste `1 cup brown sugar` alone, you get the unpacked row and a `density: loose` annotation. Salt has three rows — table salt, kosher salt, and sea salt — at 292 g, 240 g, and 273 g per cup respectively. Picking the wrong one is a 20 percent flavor swing, which is bigger than the difference between salted and unsalted butter in most cookie recipes.

## The sub-linear seasoning curve is why doubling does not ruin dinner

Scaling seasonings by a flat multiplier is the most common scaling mistake in home baking. Salt, spices, and extracts are perceptually sub-linear — doubling the recipe does not double the perceived saltiness. The tool ships a smart seasoning curve: a 2× scale up applies a 1.8× multiplier to seasoning rows, a 3× scale up applies ~2.5×, and the curve follows `multiplier^0.85`. The underlying reasoning is that salt perception is roughly logarithmic — your tongue's response to salt saturates as concentration rises, so doubling the salt in the recipe only adds about 0.85 doublings to perceived saltiness. When the curve kicks in, the output keeps both numbers visible: `1 tsp salt` scaled 2× shows as `1 4/5 tsp (linear: 2 tsp)` so you can see both the perceptually-corrected value and what a naive linear scale would have given you. This is the most useful UX detail in the entire tool — it is one thing to apply a curve, it is another to let the user override it without hunting through a hidden settings panel. The seasoning classification is heuristic, not a tagged database. The tool flags lines whose ingredient noun matches a list of 14 seasoning-ish keys: salt, pepper, baking soda, baking powder, cinnamon, nutmeg, vanilla, almond extract, etc. A line like `2 tsp cinnamon` becomes a seasoning row automatically; a line like `2 tsp cocoa powder` does not, because cocoa is a structural ingredient even at 2 tsp per batch. Disabling the curve is a single checkbox (`Smart seasoning scaling` in the options panel). Power users who know their oven and their salt brand can flip it off and get straight linear scaling for every row, including salt. The checkbox defaults to on because the failure mode of straight-linear seasoning scaling is worse than the failure mode of sub-linear correction.

## Why the output is in kitchen fractions, not decimals

Kitchen measurements are not decimals. `0.375 cup` is not a thing — `6 tablespoons` is. The output format decision is where most home-cook tools go off the rails. When the output unit is `auto` (the default) or `us`, the tool renders quantities as kitchen fractions: `0.375 cup` becomes `6 tbsp`, `0.5 cup` becomes `1/2 cup`, `0.25 tsp` becomes `1/4 tsp`, and `1.33 cup` becomes `1 1/3 cup`. The fraction chooser picks the smallest kitchen-friendly denominator — eighths for cups, thirds for tablespoons, sixteenths for teaspoons — and falls back to mixed numbers when the value exceeds 1. Metric output (`grams`, `milliliters`) skips the fraction step entirely and renders decimals with sensible rounding: `1 tsp (5 g)` becomes `5 g`, `1 cup butter (227 g)` becomes `227 g`. The kitchen-fraction renderer only activates for volume or count outputs where the fraction form is meaningful — grams and milliliters are already in their natural unit. The `auto` mode is the smart default: it keeps the original unit if the original was volume (cup, tbsp, tsp), and it converts to metric if the original was a weight (g, oz, lb). This means a recipe pasted in mixed US-and-metric form keeps its original units per line, and the output is internally consistent per line. Range inputs (`1-2 tsp`) render in fraction form as `1 1/2 tsp (range: 1-2)` so the kitchen ambiguity is preserved. Stripping the range would lose information; rendering the full range as two separate quantities would balloon the output. The midpoint-with-range call is the right compromise.

## What the parser actually pulls out of a single line

Most recipe scalers stop at finding the first number and treating it as the quantity. This one looks at four pieces per line — and gets three of them right even when the input is messy. Mixed-number detection: a line like `2 1/4 cups flour` is parsed as `2.25 cups`, not as `2` followed by garbage. The regex chain handles Unicode fractions (`½`, `⅔`, `¾`) and ASCII fractions (`1/2`, `3/4`) on the same line without confusing which numerator belongs to which denominator. Range handling: `1-2 tsp salt` becomes a midpoint (`1.5 tsp`) with the original range preserved in a sidecar so the kitchen-fraction renderer can show `1 1/2 tsp` without losing the `1-2` signal. Unit normalization: `tbsp`, `T`, `tablespoon`, `Tablespoons` all collapse to one canonical unit before density lookup. The 70+ row table that follows only has to deal with one form per unit, which is why the metric conversion stays accurate at scale. Ingredient lookup: the line's ingredient noun (`flour`, `butter`, `salt`, `vanilla extract`) is matched against the density table by exact key first, then by a fuzzy fallback for plurals and possessives (`egg` and `eggs` route to the same row). Lines with no match stay in their original unit.

## The four edge cases that break naive scalers

Scaling by a single multiplier works for the happy path. The four cases below are where naive scalers silently produce wrong output — and where this tool's parser earns its place. Scaling down below 1: scaling a 24-cookie recipe to 6 cookies (0.25×) gives `1/2 tsp salt` (linear), but the seasoning curve clamps the minimum at `0.4×` because going below the seasoning floor produces bland results. The tool surfaces the clamp in the output line so you know it happened. Scaling to 1: scaling a recipe to itself is a no-op, but the tool still re-runs the parser and the density conversion. The output is byte-identical to the input for volume-or-count lines; for lines that involve implicit rounding (`1 cup` is exactly `1 cup`, but `1/3 cup` is exactly `1/3 cup`), the round-trip is exact. Eggs and other counts: the unit is `count`, not volume, so density conversion does not apply. The tool treats `2 large eggs` as `2` units at any scale, which means `48 eggs` for a 24× recipe and `1 egg` for a 0.5× recipe. Trying to convert eggs to grams by density lookup would silently produce nonsense, so the tool refuses. Zero-servings target: the input validator rejects `targetServings: 0` with a friendly error rather than producing NaN-scaled output. This is a small thing, but it is the kind of validation that distinguishes a tool from a calculator.
