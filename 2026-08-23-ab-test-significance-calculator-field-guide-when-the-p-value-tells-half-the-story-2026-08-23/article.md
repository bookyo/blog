<h2>Why the p-value alone is not enough</h2>

A two-sided p-value answers one question: if there were no real difference between control and treatment, how rare would results this extreme look? That is the right question to ask before declaring victory, but it is silent about the *size* of the difference, the *plausible range* the effect size lives in, and the *cost* of running the experiment until it stops being noise. A p-value of 0.04 on a 0.1% lift over 24 hours of weekend traffic is statistically "significant" and operationally irrelevant. The reverse case is also common — a 10% lift that fails the 95% threshold because the sample is too small, which is a power problem, not a treatment problem. Tools that report only the p-value hide both. They also hide the multiple-comparisons trap: when five product teams run five experiments against the same control window, the chance that at least one p-value drifts below 0.05 by chance alone is roughly 23%, not 5%. The Bonferroni correction exists for this reason, and a calculator that surfaces it saves you from declaring a winner that was always noise.

<h2>Reading the inputs — proportions vs continuous means</h2>

The two test types are different computations and require different fields. For a conversion-rate experiment (checkout success rate, email signup rate, click-through rate) feed in *successes* and *visitors* per variant, not the converted percentage. The calculator runs a pooled Z-test on those counts because the variance of a proportion depends on its mean, which is exactly what the pooled formula accounts for. For a continuous metric (average order value, time on page, revenue per user) feed in the mean, the standard deviation, and the sample size per arm. The calculator runs a Welch t-test with Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom, which does not assume equal variance between control and treatment — an important detail when one arm has heavier-tailed users than the other. Most teams hit the wrong fields on first try: pasting percentages into a proportions form, or pasting min/max into a continuous form. The calculator accepts the four required fields per arm and refuses to compute until each pair is consistent.

<h2>Lift and the confidence interval</h2>

Lift — the relative change between control and treatment — is the number the marketing dashboard wants. A treatment that converts 5.2% vs control 4.8% shows 8.3% relative lift, and the [confidence interval](https://elysiatools.com/en/tools/confidence-interval) around that lift is what tells you whether the range contains zero. If the 95% interval is `+1.4% to +15.6%`, the true lift could plausibly be very small or modestly impressive, and you need more data before betting the next quarter's roadmap on it. If the interval is `+7.0% to +9.7%`, you have a result that survives most reasonable skeptical challenges. The calculator prints the lift and its CI side by side with the verdict so you cannot read past the range without seeing it. For continuous metrics the same logic applies to the absolute difference (treatment mean minus control mean) and its interval; do not compare means by ratio when the control mean is near zero, because a 0.1-second difference on a 0.5-second baseline is not "20% faster", it is meaningless.

<h2>Sample size and how long to run the experiment</h2>

Once you decide the minimum lift you care about detecting (e.g. relative lift of 5% or absolute ARPU change of $0.50), the calculator back-solves the sample size per variant required to detect that effect at 80% or 90% power with α = 0.05. The recommended experiment days are then a function of your daily traffic and the traffic split (50/50 vs 90/10). The mistake everyone makes is to launch an experiment, peek at the p-value every morning, and call it the moment it crosses the threshold. Peeking inflates false positives — the sequential-testing warning exists specifically because interim looks under α = 0.05 are no longer testing at α = 0.05. The calculator surfaces that warning and points you to always-valid sequential methods (mSPRT, group sequential) or to the simple discipline of pre-registering the sample size and waiting for it. The 80% power default catches most cases; switch to 90% only when the cost of a false positive is genuinely high (pricing changes, hero redesigns, anything that touches brand).

<h2>Bonferroni and the family-wise error rate</h2>

If five teams run experiments against a shared control on the same week, each p-value is compared against 0.05 and one of them will probably be under threshold by chance. The Bonferroni correction divides α by the number of comparisons (0.05 / 5 = 0.01 per test), which is conservative but easy to compute and explain. The calculator tells you whether the result survives the correction at the number of comparisons you declare; if you ran two variants against one control you have two comparisons, not one. Holm–Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg are less conservative and worth using when the comparison count is large. None of these corrections fix a bad design — if the experiments share users without a hash-randomized split, the calculator cannot rescue the conclusion and the verdict should be ignored.

<h2>Common input mistakes the calculator catches</h2>

Most misuses do not show up as math errors — they show up as inputs that look plausible but violate an assumption. Pasting a converted percentage into a proportions form leaves the calculator unable to compute a variance because the denominator is missing; the form will demand visitors. Pasting a 30-day average into a continuous mean, when the treatment only ran for 14 days, hides seasonality into the comparison; the calculator does not know the window you chose, so the responsibility is yours. Pasting two SEs from a chart into the SD field is a unit confusion that disproportionately inflates significance — SD is the spread of individual observations, SE is the spread of means. Pasting min/max as SD underestimates the variance and turns a noisy experiment into a "significant" one. The calculator reports the input fields verbatim in the result, which makes discrepancies easier to spot in review than they would be buried in a notebook.

<h2>Sequential testing — the peeking trap</h2>

Repeatedly checking the p-value while the experiment runs and stopping the first time it dips below 0.05 inflates the false positive rate. The naive "look once, test at α = 0.05" trick does not survive multiple looks. Always-valid sequential methods (mSPRT, mixture sequential testing, group-sequential designs with α-spending) preserve the type-I error at every look. The calculator prints a warning whenever you declare a stop date that is shorter than the recommended days, which is your cue to switch methods rather than restart the experiment. Treat the stop date as a contract written *before* the experiment launches; after the first look the temptation to keep looking is overpowering and the calculator exists partly to be the audit log.
